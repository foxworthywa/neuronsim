# Lab: The heart as a pump (HeartSim Part A)

*Paste this into a Canvas assignment with submission type **Text entry** (and, optionally, **File

---

**Time:** about 40–45 minutes. Use a laptop or desktop if you can, with sound on (headphones help).

**Open the simulation:** [HeartSim, Part A](https://foxworthywa.github.io/neuronsim/heart.html)

### What to do

1. Work through all six scenes in order. Each scene asks you to **predict** before you see the
   result. Predict honestly: your first answers are recorded, but they are graded for completion,
   not for being right. Wrong predictions that you then fix are how this lab works.
2. On the last screen, type your name and click **Copy lab record**.
3. In this assignment, paste your **whole** lab record (including the line with the check code).
   Do not edit it; an edited record will not match its code.
4. Below the record, answer the four questions. Use numbers from **your own** record and
   simulation where the question asks for them. 3–5 sentences each.

### Questions

**Q1. Valves.** In scene 3 you squeezed the ventricle yourself. Explain, step by step, what
opened and shut the bicuspid and aortic valves. Your answer should explain why there are two
moments in every beat when both valves are shut, and what happens to ventricular volume and
pressure during each.

**Q2. The cardiac cycle.** A classmate says, "The heart sounds are made by the heart muscle
contracting, and the atria pump most of the blood into the ventricles." Using what you saw in
scene 4 (the traces, the sound marks and the volume curve), correct both claims.

**Q3. Your measurements.** Report the EDV, ESV, stroke volume and ejection fraction you measured
in scene 5. Then use your scene 6 results to explain why giving 500 mL of IV fluid changed the
stroke volume even though nothing changed the heart muscle or the heart rate. Name the principle.

**Q4. Apply it.** A patient has a heart attack that damages much of the left ventricle. Using your
scene 6 results, explain why this patient may become short of breath. Trace the blood from the
right ventricle onward, and include what happened to the two ventricles' outputs over time.
