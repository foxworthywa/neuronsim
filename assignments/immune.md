### What to do

1. **Before you open the simulation**, do Part A of the handout (a drawing and one question).
   There are no wrong answers in Part A: it is graded for being done.
2. Work through all thirteen scenes. At each scene number on the handout, stop and fill in that
   box. **Write your prediction before you press each run button.** The simulation's own
   prediction questions are not graded, so guess honestly.
3. You can leave and come back. The numbered circles at the top of the simulation take you
   straight to any scene, so you can redo a scene later to check a number or an idea. Your
   progress is saved in the browser you are using: if you close the page, open it again **on the
   same computer and browser** and choose **Continue**. (On a shared computer, the next group
   should choose **Start again**.)
4. On the last screen, copy **your results** and type **your name** (in a group, everyone's names,
   separated by commas) to get your **completion code**. Paste the results onto the handout (or
   attach them) and write the code in the box in scene 13.
5. Answer Part C in full sentences, using **your own** numbers from the simulation. Explain *why*,
   not just *what*: the points are for reasoning that links cause to effect.
6. Upload one completed handout as a PDF or as clear photos (one per group, or your own if you
   worked alone).

Your numbers will not match another group's exactly. The simulation gives each group one of twelve
slightly different people, as real patients differ.

### The patients

**Priya Nair, 20,** a nursing student, scrapes her knee on a trail run. The knee becomes red, warm
and swollen; on day 3 she finds a tender lump in her groin; by day 9 or 10 she is well. Six months
later she scrapes the same knee and barely notices. **Elena Cruz, 21,** her roommate, catches
influenza and is ill for a week; Priya, vaccinated in October, is not. Scene 12 adds two short
cases: **Mr. Hall, 58,** on chemotherapy, and **Ms. Adeyemi, 34,** with untreated HIV infection.
