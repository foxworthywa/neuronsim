# IMMUNE_MODULE.md — the immune response

**Status:** built. `immune/` (engine, app, views, lesson), published as `immune.html`;
`tests/immune.test.js` (physiology, 27 tests) and `npm run test:immune` (performs every step in
Chromium); the handout is `docs/worksheet/ImmuneSim-handout.docx` / `.pdf` (generated by
`docs/worksheet/make-immune-worksheet.js`) and the Canvas page is `assignments/immune.md`. The
free-play lab and the later Part on misfires are still design. The sections below are the plan;
where the build differs, see "As built" and "Decisions" at the end.

A fourth BIO 142 module, built like EndoSim: its own folder (`immune/`), its own engine, the shared
lesson runner in `lib/`, patient cases running through it, a results screen with a group completion
code, and a group worksheet as the graded work. It follows `PEDAGOGY.md` unchanged: predict →
observe → explain, causes before names, nothing drawn that the model did not compute, Continue only
when the question is answered and the activity done.

**Length: about 90 minutes**, in two halves with a marked break (Part A ≈ 55 min, Part B ≈ 35 min).
The extra time compared with EndoSim is spent on fewer ideas done more thoroughly (each idea is
predicted, seen, then used again in a new setting), not on more content.

## The mental model the module builds

Students meet the immune system as a list of cells and molecules. What they lack is a model that
says why those parts exist. The module builds one from seven claims, each of which the student
discovers by experiment and then reuses:

1. **It is a race.** Pathogens multiply exponentially, so a defence is judged by when it arrives.
2. **There are two ways to recognise an invader.** A few fixed detectors, the same on every
   phagocyte, fit features shared by many microbes: immediate, everywhere, the same every time
   (innate). Millions of different receptors, each carried by a handful of cells, fit one shape
   each: exact, but the right cells must be found and multiplied, which takes days (adaptive).
3. **Selection, not instruction.** The body does not design a receptor after meeting a pathogen.
   The fitting cell already exists; the pathogen picks it out, and it divides.
4. **Where the pathogen is decides the weapon.** In the fluid between cells, antibodies can reach
   it. Inside a cell they cannot, and the infected cell itself has to be killed.
5. **T cells read display windows.** B cells and antibodies bind whole, free antigen. T cells see
   only fragments held out on another cell's surface: "what I am making inside" on every cell
   (MHC I, read by cytotoxic T cells), "what I swallowed" on the presenting cells (MHC II, read by
   helper T cells).
6. **The helper T cell licenses both arms.** Neither antibody production nor killer T cells get
   going without it. That second key is a safety catch, and losing it loses both arms.
7. **Memory is a changed library.** After the fight, the selected clones stay enlarged, so the
   second response starts days ahead. A vaccine makes that change without the disease.

Everything else in the chapter (complement, the antibody classes, cytokine names, lymphoid organ
histology) is left to the textbook. See "Vocabulary budget".

## Why a model, and why this order

Immunity cannot be run as a wet lab in A&P: the events are microscopic, they happen in three
places at once, and they take two weeks. A model compresses the two weeks into a minute and shows
the wound, the lymph node and the blood together.

**A bacterium first, then a virus.** A bacterium living between cells needs only phagocytes,
helper T cells and antibodies, so Part A can build claims 1–3 and 7 without cytotoxic T cells or
MHC I. Part B then asks the question Part A cannot answer (what if the pathogen is inside your own
cells?) and the second arm arrives as the solution to a problem the student has just seen.

**The student is the controller first**, as in HeartSim and EndoSim:

- Scene 3: the student *is the alarm*, opening the vessel and calling neutrophils by hand before
  the macrophages do it automatically.
- Scene 5: the student *is the selector*, hunting through the library for the one lymphocyte that
  fits, and finds out how rare it is.
- Scene 11: the student *is the cytotoxic T cell*, reading display windows and deciding which of
  the body's own cells to kill.

**Three things stay on screen for the whole lab**, so that scenes add to one picture instead of
replacing it:

- **The infection timeline:** one graph, days on the x-axis, counts on a log y-axis, with the same
  four traces throughout (pathogen, phagocytes at the site, pathogen-specific lymphocytes,
  antibody). It plays the part the voltage trace plays in NeuronSim. By the end the student can
  sketch how it changes for a second exposure, a vaccine, or a missing cell type.
- **The three-place strip:** site of infection · lymph node · blood, with cells and antigen drawn
  moving between them. It answers "where is this happening?", which the textbook figures rarely do.
- **One visual language for recognition:** every recognition event is a shape fitting a shape. A
  phagocyte's pattern receptor is the same shape on every phagocyte. Each lymphocyte's receptor is
  different. An MHC molecule is a holder with a fragment in it, and a T-cell receptor fits the
  fragment and the holder together.

## The model

One engine (`immune/engine.js`), sharing the browser/CommonJS wrapper, the event queue
(`takeEvents`) and the test harness with the other engines. Semi-quantitative, equations
invisible (`PEDAGOGY.md` §9). **Time is in hours.** Large populations are continuous; lymphocyte
clones are whole numbers of cells that divide and die by chance, from a seeded random number
generator so that tests are repeatable and each group's run can differ.

### The site: bacteria, phagocytes and inflammation

- **Bacteria** grow exponentially toward a ceiling and are killed by phagocytes. Killing follows
  the published competition between first-order growth and second-order killing: bacteria fall
  only when the phagocyte concentration exceeds a critical value, whatever the number of bacteria
  (Li et al., 2002, [doi:10.1073/pnas.122244799](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.122244799); in
  tissue, Li et al., 2004, [doi:10.1084/jem.20040725](https://doi.org/10.1084/jem.20040725)). That
  one rule gives scene 3 (the alarm must bring enough neutrophils), scene 4 (a larger dose is not
  what defeats the innate response; too few neutrophils is) and scene 12 (neutropenia).
- **Resident macrophages** are few and fixed in number. They kill a little and release the
  **alarm signal** in proportion to the bacteria they detect; the signal decays within hours.
- **The vessel** responds to the alarm: flow (redness, heat) and leak (swelling) rise, and
  **neutrophils** leave the blood at a rate set by the alarm and by the blood neutrophil count.
  Neutrophils live about a day at the site; the dead ones are the pus.
- **Antibody** reaches the site from the blood in proportion to the leak, and multiplies the
  phagocytes' kill rate by how well it fits (opsonisation). Without phagocytes it kills nothing.
- **Tissue damage** accumulates from bacteria and from neutrophil activity, and heals slowly.

### The messenger

**Dendritic cells** at the site take up antigen in proportion to the pathogen load and reach the
node about 18–24 h later. The amount of antigen in the node is what lymphocytes are selected on.
A vaccine is antigen placed at the site with growth rate zero, plus an alarm signal.

### The library

- **Shape space:** every receptor and every antigen is a point in a small shape space, and binding
  strength falls off with the distance between them, as in the shape-space models of the
  repertoire (Perelson and Oster, 1979,
  [doi:10.1016/0022-5193(79)90275-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5193(79)90275-3)). The same
  numbers draw the shapes on screen, so a receptor that looks like a fit is one.
- **Three libraries** (B cells, helper T cells, cytotoxic T cells), each a few thousand clones of
  one to a few cells, generated at random **before** any pathogen is chosen. The width of binding
  is tuned so that any random antigen is fitted well by one to five clones. Real naive frequencies
  are of the order of one cell in 10⁵ to 10⁶ (20–200 naive helper T cells per mouse for a given
  epitope: Moon et al., 2007,
  [doi:10.1016/j.immuni.2007.07.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.immuni.2007.07.007)); the screen
  says the library is scaled down.
- **Self screening:** clones that fit any of a fixed set of self shapes are deleted when the
  library is built. A lab switch can turn this off, which is the hook for a later autoimmunity Part.
- **Selection and expansion:** a clone divides at a rate set by its fit × the antigen available ×
  its licence, up to about one division every 8–10 h. Helper T clones are licensed by dendritic
  cells that arrived with an alarm signal; B and cytotoxic T clones are licensed by the number of
  activated helper T cells. Nothing in the code says which clone wins.
- **Effectors:** B cells become plasma cells that secrete antibody, IgM first and IgG after a few
  days of help (half-lives about 5 and 21 days). Cytotoxic T cells travel to the site.
- **Contraction and memory:** once antigen is gone, about 90–95 % of the expanded clone dies over
  one to two weeks and the rest persist as memory cells, which respond to less antigen and start
  dividing sooner. This follows what is measured for cytotoxic T cells after a viral infection,
  including no response of those memory cells to an unrelated virus (Murali-Krishna et al., 1998,
  [doi:10.1016/s1074-7613(00)80470-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1074-7613(00)80470-7)). A small
  population of long-lived plasma cells keeps a low level of IgG.

The secondary response is therefore not a separate mechanism: it is the same rules run on a
library in which one clone starts hundreds of times larger.

### The virus

The standard target-cell-limited model of influenza (healthy cells, infected cells, free virus,
with a delay before an infected cell releases virus), with the published human parameters as the
starting point: infected cells live about 11 h, free virus has a half-life of about 3 h, and one
infected cell gives rise to about 22 more (Baccam et al., 2006,
[doi:10.1128/JVI.01623-05](https://doi.org/10.1128/JVI.01623-05)). Added to it:

- **Antibody** blocks free virus from entering cells (neutralisation) and has no effect on
  infected cells.
- **Infected cells display viral fragments on MHC I;** cytotoxic T cells kill infected cells at a
  rate set by their number and their fit.
- **An innate brake** (interferon in the published model) slows spread in the first days without
  clearing the infection. It is in the engine; whether it is named on screen is an open question.
- **Fever and aching** are computed from the alarm signals, not from the virus count.

### Knockouts and patients

Each is one parameter: blood neutrophil count, phagocyte function, B-cell library on or off,
cytotoxic T library on or off, helper T count (as a fraction of normal, shown as a CD4 count),
alarm strength (prednisone), self screening. Scene 12's switches, the two short cases and the
free-play lab all use these.

### Engine API (sketch)

```js
const body = new Immune({ seed: 7 });            // healthy adult; libraries built now, before any pathogen
body.infect('bacterium', { dose: 1e4, strain: 'A' });   // also 'virus'; strain sets the antigen shape
body.advance(dt);                                // dt in hours
body.site        // {bacteria, virus, infected, macrophages, neutrophils, alarm, flow, leak, damage, antibody}
body.node        // {antigen, size, clones: [{kind:'B'|'Th'|'Tc', shape, fit, naive, effector, memory}]}
body.blood       // {neutrophils, cd4, IgM, IgG}
body.signs       // {redness, heat, swelling, pain, fever}
body.set('neutrophils', 0.1); body.set('helperT', 0.15); body.set('alarm', 0.3);
body.vaccinate('virus', { strain: 'A' }); body.giveAntibody({ strain: 'A', amount });
body.labPanel(['ANC', 'CD4', 'IgM', 'IgG']);     // values with units, ranges and H/L flags
body.takeEvents();   // [{type:'neutrophilsArrive', t}, {type:'antigenInNode', t}, {type:'firstAntibody', t}, {type:'cleared', t}, …]
```

### Speed

About one simulated day per 2–3 seconds for whole-infection runs, slowed to about an hour per
second for scenes 2, 3 and 11, where the student acts by hand. The clock and calendar are always
shown, and the speed control is labelled in plain words.

## Acceptance tests (`tests/immune.test.js`)

- With no defences, bacteria rise about 100-fold in 10 h and reach the ceiling within two days.
- A small dose is cleared by resident macrophages with no measurable rise in flow or leak.
- At the standard dose the innate response alone holds the bacteria within a tenfold band for a
  week without clearing them; with the alarm at 30 % they escape.
- Innate only: a second exposure a month later gives the same curve as the first to within 5 %.
- For 100 random antigens against the same library, at least one clone fits each, and the best-
  fitting clone differs between antigens.
- The clone that expands most is the one with the best fit, without the test naming it.
- A full response to the standard bacterial dose: antibody first detectable on day 4–7, clearance
  by day 10, node size peaking between.
- Antibody with no phagocytes present kills no bacteria; with phagocytes, the kill rate rises
  several-fold.
- No helper T cells: no antibody, no cytotoxic T expansion, innate curves unchanged.
- A B cell bound to a self shape receives no licence.
- Second exposure to the same strain: antibody rising by day 3, peak pathogen load at least
  100-fold lower than the first time, signs within the normal band.
- Second exposure to a different strain: within 20 % of a first-exposure curve.
- Virus with antibody given after infection is established: free virus falls at least tenfold,
  infected cells are still present two days later. Antibody given before exposure: no infection.
- Virus with no cytotoxic T cells: clearance delayed by at least several days or not reached.
- Neutrophils at 10 %: bacteria escape, and the redness, swelling and pus values stay below half
  of normal.
- Vaccine: pathogen and infected-cell counts stay at zero; exposure 14 days later resembles a
  second exposure; exposure on day 3 resembles a first.
- Injected antibody protects at once, decays with the IgG half-life, and leaves the library
  unchanged.
- Titre patterns: never exposed (IgM −, IgG −), day 7 of a first infection (IgM +), six months
  after infection or vaccination (IgM −, IgG +).
- The same seed gives the same run.

## Visual style (extends `VISUAL_STYLE.md`)

- Same background, panels and trace style as the other modules.
- One fixed colour per cell type and one for antibody, never reused for an ion or a hormone, and
  the same on the timeline as on the stage (to be chosen and listed here).
- Cells drawn on stage are a sample of the model's populations, at a stated scale; a count beside
  each view gives the real number.
- Receptors and antigens are drawn from their shape-space coordinates. Pattern receptors are one
  shape on every phagocyte. MHC holders are one outline for class I and another for class II.
- The timeline's y-axis is logarithmic and stays the same from scene 2 to scene 13.

## Accessibility (design in, not retrofit)

As EndoSim: keyboard-operable controls, an `aria-live` narrator, nothing shown by colour alone,
a reduced-motion setting. The shape hunt in scene 5 and the window reading in scene 11 must be
doable from the keyboard and must not depend on speed.

## Out of scope for this module

Everything under "Left to the textbook" above; antibiotics and antivirals; allergy, autoimmunity,
transplant rejection and Rh incompatibility (a later Part, see the build plan); cancer immunity;
the molecular detail of receptor gene rearrangement; scoring and accounts.

Each milestone is usable in a course on its own.

## Decisions (instructor, Nov 2026)

1. **One skill:** the module is built around the race and selection, not around a tour of the
   parts.
2. **Detail left to the textbook:** complement, the antibody classes, cytokine names and lymphatic
   anatomy.
3. **Length:** about 90 minutes, used to secure the key ideas, not to add content.
4. **Pathogens:** a bacterium first, then a virus; named *Streptococcus pyogenes* and influenza A.
5. **Blood typing** is not required.
6. **One lab session**, with a marked break after scene 9. Students can jump to any scene with the
   numbered circles, and progress is saved in the browser so a scene can be revisited later.
7. **Grading:** a handout that students turn in, as for the other sims. Groups in the lab; online
   students do the same exercise, usually alone, so the completion code works for one name or
   several and "talk first" items have a written alternative.
8. **Textbook:** McKinley, *Anatomy & Physiology: An Integrative Approach*. The sim uses its terms:
   B-lymphocyte, helper T-lymphocyte, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte, antigen-presenting cell, MHC class I
   and II, innate and adaptive immunity. (Terms were matched from memory of the text; check them
   against the edition in use.)
9. **Clarity over depth** (instructor: these are beginning undergraduates). Left to judgement and
   decided as follows: IgM and IgG are **not** named, and there is one antibody; self-tolerance
   gets **one** step (scene 5); interferon is **not** named.

## As built (differences from the plan above)

- **Thirteen scenes, 66 steps.** The scene list is as planned. Changes inside scenes:
  - *Scene 1* opens with anatomy beside function (instructor review, Nov 2026): the whole-body
    screen shows an anatomical figure of the lymphatic system ("Anatomy of the Lymphatic System",
    OpenStax College, CC BY 3.0; `immune/img/CREDITS.md`) on the left and the lab's simplified
    diagram on the right. Two steps orient the student: clicking an axillary lymph node on the
    lab's diagram after finding the clusters in the figure, and reading the figure's close-ups
    (lymphatic capillaries; what is inside a lymph node). The figure opens in a larger window
    when clicked. The copy used is the 2013 release on Wikimedia Commons, because OpenStax now
    licenses the images on its own site CC BY-NC-SA.
  - *Scene 4* compares a first exposure, a second exposure a month later and a different strain,
    all with innate defenses only. The first step says outright that a real person also has
    B- and T-lymphocytes and that they are switched off here (instructor review, Nov 2026). The planned "twice the dose" run was dropped: in the model, as
    in the published experiments, what decides the outcome is the number of neutrophils at the
    site, not the dose.
  - *Scene 5* opens by listing what is new in the picture (instructor review, Nov 2026): a
    dendritic cell, drawn at the site as its own purple star-shaped cell and labelled, distinct
    from the blue macrophages; the lymph vessel; the lymph node with its three kinds of
    lymphocyte; the blood; and the two new curves on the timeline. The same purple star travels
    along the lymph vessel and appears, large, in scene 7.
    A second step sets the macrophage beside the dendritic cell in a three-row table (both swallow
    bacteria; one stays to kill and raise the alarm, the other carries antigen to the node) and
    names the antigen-presenting cell. Scene 7's name box keeps the textbook's list of
    antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages, B-lymphocytes) and says how they
    divide the work.
  - *Scene 6* asks about the lag as "about a week" (the B-lymphocyte clone reaches a thousand
    cells around day 7, because it waits for helper T-lymphocytes, which scene 7 then explains).
  - *Scene 7* has one self-tolerance sentence in a feedback, not a separate experiment.
  - *Scene 12* is a 4 × 2 table (neutrophils, B-lymphocytes, cytotoxic T-lymphocytes, helper
    T-lymphocytes × bacteria, virus); the planned "second exposure" column was dropped. The
    wiring exercise is a second small table (who needs the go-ahead from whom; which pathogens
    each deals with).
  - *Scene 13* ends with a titer report for three people that makes one point: a titer measures
    antibody, not memory.
- **Files.** `immune/engine.js` (the model), `immune/app.js` (state, clock, the timeline),
  `immune/anatomy.js` (the anatomical figure as a data URI, generated by
  `node immune/img/embed.js`), `immune/views.js` (six views: `body`, `places`, `node`, `handshake`, `dish`, `windows`),
  `immune/lesson-a.js` and `immune/lesson-b.js` (the two Parts, results and completion code).
- **Lymphocyte clones are continuous numbers, not whole cells dividing by chance.** A run is
  therefore exactly repeatable. Variety between groups comes from the seed instead: each group
  gets one of twelve people (libraries), so numbers differ a little between groups (first
  infection cleared on day 8.8 to 10.9; influenza on day 9.4 to 12.1). `?seed=7` in the address
  fixes the person.
- **The repertoire** is 36 × 36 clones per library, one in each cell of a grid in shape space at
  a random place within it, so every antigen is fitted by one to a few clones and none by many.
- **Cytotoxic T-lymphocytes are selected only by antigen made inside cells,** so they respond to
  the virus but not to bacteria between cells or to the vaccine's protein. The vaccine therefore
  protects through antibody and B/helper memory, as an inactivated vaccine does.
- **Virus model.** The target-cell-limited model, plus regrowth of the lining, an unnamed innate
  brake, and direct cell-to-cell spread that antibody cannot block. The last is what makes
  "antibody cannot clear an established infection" come out of the model: with ready-made
  antibody, free virus falls about a hundredfold and several thousand cells stay infected.
- **Plasma cells that come from memory cells count for four times the antibody** (standing for the
  better-binding antibody of a secondary response), so the second antibody peak is higher than
  the first, as in the textbook figure.
- **Signs.** Redness and heat follow blood flow; swelling follows the leak, which neutrophils
  amplify; pus is dead neutrophils; fever follows the alarm sensed by macrophages and the number
  of infected cells, and lags by hours. A patient with few neutrophils therefore has a fever with
  little swelling and no pus.
- **Unjudged guesses.** The shared lesson runner (`lib/lesson.js`) gained one option flag,
  `neutral`, for opening guesses: the choice is recorded and acknowledged ("Noted") without being
  called right. Other modules are unaffected.
- **Saved progress.** Answers, results and tables are kept in the browser's local storage. On
  return the student chooses Continue or Start again (the latter for shared computers).
- **Measured with the default model (person 7):** 1,000 bacteria unopposed for 24 h: 62 million.
  Innate only: neutrophils first at about 10 h; bacteria peak 150 million at day 1.3, then held
  near 6 million. Critical number of neutrophils at the site: 2,000. Prednisone (alarm at 30 %):
  726 million bacteria at 48 h. Fitting B-lymphocyte clones: 3 of 1,296. First infection: first
  antibody day 5.8, cleared day 8.9; lymphocytes that fit 9 → 58 thousand → 3,500 by day 42.
  Second infection: cleared day 1.3, peak 1.9 million bacteria, swelling 11 % against 69 %.
  Dish, 6 h from 100 thousand: 1.6 million alone or with antibody; 790 thousand with phagocytes;
  0 with both. Influenza: 18 thousand cells infected by day 3; cytotoxic T-lymphocytes arrive
  day 6.6; cleared day 9.4. Vaccine: antibody none on day 3, about 1,200 units on day 14; exposure
  at two weeks infects no cells; on day 3 or with a changed strain, 17 to 18 thousand.
